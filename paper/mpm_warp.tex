\section{What this is}

The four MLS-MPM operators --- \code{mpm\_strain}, \code{mpm\_scatter}, \code{mpm\_grid\_update},
\code{mpm\_gather} --- now exist in two forms that a spec selects by name on the
\code{implementation} axis:

\begin{itemize}
\item \gd{\code{default}} --- the prior MPM. Pure PyTorch, dimension-generic (2D and 3D from one
  body), runs on CPU and on any CUDA device, and \textbf{differentiable in use, not merely in
  principle}: \code{engine.run(\ldots, grad=True)} keeps the autograd tape across the rollout
  (\code{engine.py:1498}) and \code{discovery\_cardio\_mpm/train.py} calls \code{loss.backward()}
  through exactly these four operators. This is the reference: every claim below is measured against
  it.
\item \gd{\code{warp}} --- the refactor. \code{mpm\_scatter} and \code{mpm\_gather} become one NVIDIA
  Warp kernel each: one CUDA thread per particle, and the particle's whole 27-node loop runs inside
  that thread with the running sums held in registers (\S4). 3D and CUDA only.
  \code{mpm\_strain} and \code{mpm\_grid\_update} are unchanged and still run the \code{default}
  bodies.
\end{itemize}

\code{paper/mpm\_warp\_code.pdf} is the companion to this note: \textbf{every line of both versions
of all four operators}, extracted from the source at build time by
\code{tools/mpm\_code\_appendix.py} rather than transcribed, with the line spans stamped on each
listing. It also states plainly which two operators have no \code{warp} twin yet.

They are the same mechanism and the same equations, so they sit on the \code{implementation} axis
rather than the \code{model} axis: a spec that swaps one for the other is asserting that the biology
did not change. That assertion is what \S6 tests.

\paragraph{The headline.} On \Ntrace{} particles, seven substeps per frame, one RTX A6000:
\bd{1150.8\,ms/frame} for \code{default} against \gd{79.4\,ms/frame} for \code{warp}
(\gd{14.5$\times$}), and \gd{52.2\,ms/frame} with the substep loop additionally captured as a CUDA
graph (\gd{22.0$\times$}). Peak allocated memory falls from 2.94\,GiB to 0.35\,GiB, which is the
reason for the speedup and not a side effect of it.

\section{The cycle, and what each operator owes}

MLS-MPM carries the material on particles $p$ and solves on a background grid $i$ that is thrown
away every substep. Per substep, with $\Delta x$ the cell size, $w_{ip}$ the quadratic B-spline
weight of node $i$ at particle $p$, and $\mathbf{x}_{ip}=\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_p$:

\begin{align}
\textbf{strain}\quad & \mathbf{F}_p \leftarrow (\mathbf{I}+\Delta t\,\mathbf{C}_p)\,\mathbf{F}_p \\
\textbf{scatter}\quad & m_i = \sum_p w_{ip} m_p,
  \qquad (m\mathbf{v})_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\big[m_p\mathbf{v}_p + \mathbf{A}_p\,\mathbf{x}_{ip}\big] \\
& \mathbf{A}_p = -\tfrac{4\Delta t}{\Delta x^{2}}V_p^{0}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_p + m_p\mathbf{C}_p,
  \qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma}_p = 2\mu(\mathbf{F}_p-\mathbf{R}_p)\mathbf{F}_p^{\!\top}
   + \lambda J(J-1)\mathbf{I} \\
\textbf{grid}\quad & \mathbf{v}_i = (m\mathbf{v})_i / \max(m_i,\epsilon),
  \quad\text{then gravity, walls, plates, surface tension} \\
\textbf{gather}\quad & \mathbf{v}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{v}_i,
  \qquad \mathbf{C}_p = \tfrac{4}{\Delta x}\sum_i w_{ip}\,
    \mathbf{v}_i \otimes (\mathbf{o}_i - \mathbf{f}_p),
  \qquad \mathbf{x}_p \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_p + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_p
\end{align}

$\mathbf{F}_p$ is the \textbf{deformation gradient} --- one $D{\times}D$ matrix per particle
($3{\times}3$ in 3D, so \code{p.F} is $[N,3,3]$, initialised to the identity at
\code{entities.py:238}). It is the Jacobian $\partial\mathbf{x}/\partial\mathbf{X}$ of the map from a
particle's reference position to its current one, so it carries the accumulated stretch, shear and
rotation since $t=0$; strain and stress are derived \emph{from} it, and it is not itself a strain
tensor. $\mathbf{R}_p$ is its orthogonal polar factor (the rotation part) and $J=\det\mathbf{F}_p$ is
the volume ratio, $1$ for undeformed material;
$\mathbf{o}_i\in\{0,1,2\}^3$ is the node's offset within the stencil and $\mathbf{f}_p$ the
particle's fractional cell position, so $\mathbf{o}_i-\mathbf{f}_p$ is $\mathbf{x}_{ip}$ in cell
units. \textbf{The stencil is $3^3=27$ nodes, not $3^2=9$}: the quadratic B-spline has support of
three cells \emph{per axis}, and in three dimensions the tensor product of three axes is $27$. Every
per-particle cost in this note carries that factor of $27$.

\paragraph{The contract both implementations sign.} \code{mpm\_scatter} reads
\code{pos, vel, C, F, mass, mu, la, pvol} and the parent's acceleration, and accumulates into
\code{mpm\_grid.m} and \code{.mv} \emph{in place}. It must \textbf{not} touch \code{F} --- an earlier
attempt fused the strain update into the scatter kernel for free, and advanced \code{F} twice per
substep; the grid mass still agreed to eleven digits while \code{F} disagreed at $3.3\times10^{-4}$.
\code{mpm\_gather} reads \code{mpm\_grid.v}, writes \code{pos}, \code{vel} and \code{C} in place, and
leaves dormant particles (\code{occ}$=0$, the growth reservoir) exactly where they were.

\section{\code{default}, line by line: what each line allocates}

The gather is the shorter of the two and makes the point completely. This is
\code{mpm\_ops.py} lines 790--845, with the tensors folded in; $N$ is the particle count, $S=27$,
$D=3$.

\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none]
offsets = stencil_offsets(D, dev); S = offsets.shape[0]          # [27,3]   memoised, free
X, V = p.get("pos"), p.get("vel")                                # views    free
fx, weight, flat = bspline(X, inv_dx, offsets, g.shape, periodic)
    base   = (X * inv_dx - 0.5).floor().long()                   # [N,3]    i64   22.7 MB
    fx     = X * inv_dx - base.float()                           # [N,3]    f32   11.3 MB
    w      = stack(3 quadratic terms)                            # [N,3,3]  f32   34.0 MB
    weight = ones(N,S); for k: weight *= w[:, oidx[:,k], k]       # [N,27]   f32  102.1 MB x6
    gpos   = base[:,None,:] + oidx[None]                         # [N,27,3] i64  612.4 MB
    comps  = [gpos[...,k].clamp(0, shape[k]-1) for k in 0..2]    # [N,27]   i64  204.1 MB x3
    flat   = ((c0 * ny) + c1) * nz + c2                          # [N,27]   i64  204.1 MB x4
gvn = g.v[flat].view(p.n, S, D)                                  # [N,27,3] f32  306.2 MB
new_V = (weight[..., None] * gvn).sum(1)                         # [N,27,3] f32  306.2 MB
dpos_grid = offsets[None] - fx[:, None, :]                       # [N,27,3] f32  306.2 MB
new_C = 4*inv_dx*(weight[...,None,None] *
        (gvn[...,:,None] @ dpos_grid[...,None,:])).sum(1)        # [N,27,3,3] f32 918.5 MB x2
...
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The last line is the whole story. \code{gvn[...,:,None] @ dpos\_grid[...,None,:]} is $N\times27$
independent $3{\times}1$ by $1{\times}3$ outer products; PyTorch has no way to express ``form this
$3{\times}3$, weight it, add it to a running sum, discard it''. It must \textbf{materialise all
$N\times27$ of them}, write \gd{918.5\,MB} to global memory, read it back to multiply by
\code{weight}, write \gd{another 918.5\,MB}, read \emph{that} back to reduce over the stencil axis,
and only then produce the $[N,3,3]$ that is actually wanted --- \gd{34.0\,MB}. The useful output is
\gd{1.8\%} of the traffic spent producing it.

\section{\code{warp}, line by line: the same arithmetic, nowhere to put it}

The Warp kernel is one thread per particle. Everything the table above lists as a tensor is, here, a
local variable --- and a local variable in a CUDA thread is a register, which is not memory.

\paragraph{Nothing is ever 27 wide, and that is the point.} It would be wrong to picture 27 nodes
held at once: a thread has $\approx$64 registers to work with, nowhere near enough for 27 copies of
(weight, node velocity, offset). The loop instead \emph{visits} the 27 nodes one at a time and
\textbf{accumulates}. Only two things persist across iterations --- \code{newv} (3 floats) and
\code{newC} (9 floats), twelve registers of running sum --- and each node's weight, velocity and
offset live for exactly one iteration before the next node overwrites them. The register cost is
therefore $O(1)$ in the stencil size, not $O(27)$, which is precisely what PyTorch cannot express:
having no way to say ``form this, add it to a running sum, discard it'', it must make the loop a
tensor axis and materialise all 27 slices at once.

\code{ptxas -arch=sm\_86 -O3 --verbose} on Warp's emitted PTX confirms the arithmetic worked out:
\gd{64 registers, 0 bytes stack frame, 0 bytes spill stores, 0 bytes spill loads} for both
\code{g2p} and \code{p2g\_atomic}. \textbf{Zero spill is the load-bearing number} --- had the working
set exceeded the register file, the compiler would have quietly pushed the overflow to local memory,
which is global memory wearing a different name, and the fusion would have bought nothing.

\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none]
@wp.kernel                                    # src/plexus/operators/mpm_warp.py:193
def g2p(X, STATE, C, GV, OCC, LIQ, pa, va, ngx, ngy, ngz, dx, dt, ...):
    p = wp.tid()                              # one thread per particle
    x    = X[p]                                            # vec3   -> 3 registers
    base = wp.vec3(floor(x[k]*inv_dx - 0.5) for k)         # vec3   -> 3 registers
    fx   = x*inv_dx - base                                 # vec3   -> 3 registers
    newv = wp.vec3(0,0,0)                                  # vec3   -> 3 registers
    newC = wp.mat33(0,...,0)                               # mat33  -> 9 registers
    for i in range(3):                        # the 27-node stencil, UNROLLED by the compiler
      wi = <quadratic in fx[0]>               #   the [N,3,3] `w` tensor: 1 register, reused
      for j in range(3):
        wj = <quadratic in fx[1]>
        for k in range(3):
          wk = <quadratic in fx[2]>
          w    = wi*wj*wk                     #   the [N,27] `weight` tensor: 1 register
          gv   = GV[(gi*ngy + gj)*ngz + gk]   #   the [N,27,3] `gvn` tensor: 3 registers
          dpos = wp.vec3(i-fx[0], j-fx[1], k-fx[2])   #   the [N,27,3] `dpos_grid`: 3 registers
          newv = newv + w*gv                          #   the [N,27,3] product: never exists
          newC = newC + (4*inv_dx*w)*wp.outer(gv, dpos)  # the [N,27,3,3]: NEVER EXISTS
    ...
    STATE[p, pa+0..2] = xn ; STATE[p, va+0..2] = newv ; C[p] = newC   # the only writes
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The traced \code{warp} run confirms it: \code{mpm\_scatter} and \code{mpm\_gather} do not appear in
the allocating-op trace \emph{at all}. What remains is \code{mpm\_strain} (\bpstrain\,B/particle) and
\code{mpm\_grid\_update} (\bpgridupdate\,B/particle), both still \code{default} bodies, for a total of
\gd{\ampltotalwarp$\times$} the essential traffic against \code{default}'s \bd{\ampltotal$\times$}.

\paragraph{The scatter differs in one way the gather does not.} Grid$\rightarrow$particle is a pure
read: no two threads write the same place, so there is nothing to coordinate. Particle$\rightarrow$
grid is the opposite --- many particles land on the same node, and the kernel uses
\code{wp.atomic\_add}, 108 of them per particle ($27$ nodes $\times$ (1 mass $+$ 3 momentum)). That is
why the two fusions do not cost the same and do not buy the same (\S5), and it is the remaining wall
(\S7).

\paragraph{Differentiability is not lost in principle, only unwired here.} Warp emits a backward
kernel next to every forward one --- \code{g2p\_\ldots\_cuda\_kernel\_backward} sits in the same PTX
file --- so the reason \code{warp} carries \code{DIFFERENTIABLE = False} is not that the tool cannot
do it. It is that this port launches with a plain \code{wp.launch} outside a \code{wp.Tape}, and
\code{wp.from\_torch} wraps the tensors without \code{requires\_grad}, so no backward is registered
with autograd. Wiring it up is a separate job; until it is done, anything that needs a gradient ---
\code{discovery\_cardio\_mpm}'s training loop above all --- must stay on \code{default}, and that is
a second reason (alongside byte-identity, \S6) why \code{default} is not going away.

\paragraph{One convenience worth naming.} \code{wp.mat33} is a first-class type with
\code{determinant}, \code{inverse}, \code{transpose} and \code{outer}, so the polar iteration
$\mathbf{R}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{R}^{-\top})$ is three lines. The same kernel
written in Triton needed twenty-seven hand-rolled cofactor expressions, because Triton has no matrix
type. Fewer lines is not the argument; \emph{fewer places to put a cofactor sign error} is.

\subsection{Which fusion did what}

Each operator was switched on its own, on the \Ntrace-particle benchmark, RTX A6000, 12 timed frames
after 4 warm-up frames.

\textbf{The two fusions are superlinear together.} Alone they are worth $2.5\times$ and $1.5\times$ on
the frame; together they are worth $14.5\times$, and $2.5\times1.5=3.75$, not $14.5$. The reason is in

\subsection{CUDA-graph capture: what it is, what it removes, and when it stops earning its keep}

\code{capture: true} on a schedule's substep block tells the engine to record that block's four
operators \emph{once} as a \code{torch.cuda.CUDAGraph} and then replay the recording seven times a
frame, instead of re-issuing the kernels through Python each substep. It is orthogonal to the
\code{implementation} axis: \code{default} and \code{warp} can each be captured or not, and the
benchmark tables above report both settings for exactly that reason.

\paragraph{What it removes is not launch latency.} Capture is worth \gd{1.73$\times$} at 100\,M
(7853.9 $\to$ 4551.0\,ms/frame) --- but at 4.5\,s a frame, the 28 kernel launches it elides are
nanoseconds against seconds, so launch overhead cannot be the mechanism. The memory column names it:
peak \emph{rises} \gd{30.89 $\to$ 42.81\,GiB} under capture, a 39\% increase. A captured graph
allocates from a private pool that stays resident, so \code{mpm\_strain}'s and
\code{mpm\_grid\_update}'s $[N,3,3]$ intermediates are allocated \emph{once} instead of being
malloc'd and freed seven times a frame. Capture is buying the removal of the caching allocator ---
the same thing fusion bought, for the two operators fusion has not reached.

\paragraph{Which means it has an expiry date, and that is the point.} Everything capture earns here,
it earns on behalf of the un-fused half. \code{mpm\_scatter[warp]} and \code{mpm\_gather[warp]}
allocate nothing (\S4), so there is nothing for the private pool to hold on their behalf; the
\gd{1.73$\times$} is \code{mpm\_strain}'s 313\,B and \code{mpm\_grid\_update}'s 151\,B per particle
per substep, being allocated once rather than seven times. \textbf{When those two are ported, capture's
benefit should largely evaporate while its 39\% memory cost remains} --- so the right sequence is to
fuse them and \emph{re-measure} capture, not to leave a flag on because it once helped. If it falls
to a few percent it should default to off.

\paragraph{It fails silently, and that is its real cost today.} A capture pool that does not fit does
not raise. The allocator retries, so the run sits at 100\% CPU with no output, no error and no
progress --- indefinitely. That is what a 100\,M render did on a 48\,GiB A6000: 42.8\,GiB of capture
pool, plus a live renderer, plus 3.6\,GB of recording buffers. The diagnostic is an \emph{absence}:
the engine prints \code{substep captured as a CUDA graph} when a capture completes, and that line
never appeared. \code{Plexus\_Main.py} now projects the footprint before the run --- measured at
0.42\,GiB per million particles captured against 0.309 eager --- compares it with \emph{that card's}
memory rather than against a particle count or a card name, warns when it exceeds 80\%, and offers
\code{--no-capture}. A fixed threshold would have nagged on an 80\,GiB H100, where 100\,M is
unremarkable, and stayed silent on a 24\,GiB card, where 50\,M is not.

\section{Precision}

\code{warp} is \textbf{not} byte-identical to \code{default} and cannot be, for two independent
reasons: the 27-tap sums are reassociated (the kernel accumulates sequentially, PyTorch's
\code{.sum(1)} reduces pairwise), and \code{wp.atomic\_add} in the scatter commits in whatever order
the hardware schedules. So this is an \code{implementation} with a tolerance gate, not a promotion
twin with \code{tools/mpm\_identity\_gate.py}.

Measured after 2 frames $\times$ 7 substeps on 108\,k particles, both implementations run from the
same seed:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrl@{}}
\toprule
switched & quantity & max $|\Delta|$ & max relative & reference scale \\
\midrule
gather only  & \code{pos} & $1.79\times10^{-7}$ & $4.3\times10^{-7}$ & $|x|\approx0.40$ \\
gather only  & \code{F}   & $8.34\times10^{-7}$ & $8.3\times10^{-7}$ & $|F|\approx0.33$ \\
gather only  & grid \code{m} & $3.07\times10^{-12}$ & --- & total mass identical to 6 digits \\
\midrule

both         & \code{F}   & $8.34\times10^{-7}$ & $8.3\times10^{-7}$ & $|F|\approx0.33$ \\

\noindent
Float32 has $\epsilon=1.19\times10^{-7}$, so a position of $0.40$ has an ulp of $2.98\times10^{-8}$
and the observed $1.79\times10^{-7}$ is \gd{6 ulp after 14 substeps} --- round-off, accumulated at
the rate reassociation predicts. The large \emph{relative} figures on the grid (up to
$1.2\times10^{-4}$) are on nodes whose mass is $\approx10^{-8}$: a tiny denominator, not a
disagreement. Total grid mass matches to six digits.

\paragraph{What this costs, stated plainly.} A run cannot be reproduced bit-for-bit across the two
implementations, so the \code{implementation} field is part of a run's identity and must be recorded
with it. Anything that needs byte-identity --- the promotion twins --- stays on \code{default}.

\section{The sweep, one year on: what is fastest now}

Everything above compares \code{default} against \code{warp} on \emph{one} scene at a fixed
$96^3$ grid. Two things have changed since, and both move the answer: \code{mpm\_grid\_update} now
has a \code{warp} implementation (\S8.1 below), and a defect outside the MPM operators entirely was
costing $4.4\times$ on every run in the repository.

\subsection{The aggregate, and why it dominated everything}

\code{engine.py} used to set \code{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms(True)} at import, so that two
runs of one spec agreed bit for bit. That flag reroutes CUDA \code{index\_add\_} from atomics to a
sort-and-segment kernel whose cost is driven by the longest run of \emph{duplicate} indices --- and
\code{aggregate}, reducing 570{,}760 particles into a set with a \emph{single} parent, hands it
570{,}760 copies of the index \code{0}. Measured on an RTX A6000, \code{index\_add\_} of
$[570760,3]$ into $[1,3]$:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{1}{c}{time} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{vs \code{sum(0)}} \\
\midrule
\code{index\_add\_}, deterministic & 140.98\,ms & $8{,}800\times$ \\
\code{index\_add\_}, atomics       &   1.00\,ms & $63\times$ \\
\code{sum(0)}                      &   0.016\,ms & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
On \code{si\_waterfall} those two calls were 214\,ms of a 255\,ms frame --- \textbf{84\% of the
simulation spent taking the centroid of a set with one member}, while the four MPM operators
together took 41\,ms. With one parent the index carries no information, so the segment sum
\emph{is} the sum; \code{sum(0)} is also the more accurate of the two, because a tree reduction's
error grows with $\log N$ where the sequential scan's grows with $N$ (against a float64 evaluation:
$2.1\times10^{-2}$ against $5.9\times10^{2}$ on a sum of $2.75\times10^{5}$). Determinism is now
opt-in through \code{PLEXUS\_STRICT\_DETERMINISM=1}, which the promotion gate already exported; no
spec in the corpus of 2{,}456 ever asked for it.

\subsection{Which implementation, on two scenes and two cards}

\noindent
\textbf{\code{warp} is $25$--$32\times$ \code{default}} on the A100, and the fused grid solve adds
another $1.22\times$ at 570\,k and $1.34\times$ at 5\,M --- more at the larger scene because its grid

constraint, which is exactly what \S8.1 predicted would happen once the two remaining \code{default}
operators were ported.

\code{torch\_loop27} is a third implementation of \code{mpm\_gather}, written to test whether the
$[N,27,D,D]$ temporaries are the cost rather than the arithmetic: it walks the 27 stencil offsets in
python and accumulates $[N,D]$. It is $3.70\times$ the batched torch gather on the operator
($34.20\to9.24$\,ms per call) and $1.75\times$ end to end, which localises the batched form's cost to
its intermediates and not to its indexing.

\begin{center}\footnotesize\tblScaling\end{center}

\noindent
The marginal cost is \textbf{276\,B per particle}, measured two-point, so an 80\,GiB H100 holds
$\approx$290\,M and a 267.7\,GiB B300 $\approx$970\,M. One billion particles fits with about 9\,GiB
to spare, and only with \code{PYTORCH\_CUDA\_ALLOC\_CONF=expandable\_segments:True} --- without it the
run dies asking for the 33.5\,GiB \code{F} buffer against 13.6\,GiB free and 5.6\,GiB lost to
fragmentation. \textbf{Throughput improves with size}: 2.08\,ns per particle-substep at 200\,M
against 0.86 at 1\,B, because a bigger problem hides the launch and tail effects a smaller one
cannot.

\section{What is left}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{\code{mpm\_strain} is still \code{default}}; \code{mpm\_grid\_update} no longer is
  (\S8.2), and porting it moved that operator from 42.7\% of the frame to 11.2\% --- $1.860\to
  0.328$\,ms per call, $5.67\times$. The scatter is now 58.6\% and is the next target. What follows
  was written before either change and is kept because its accounting still holds: \bpstrain\,B and \bpgridupdate\,B per particle-substep against
  \code{warp}'s zero for the other two. \code{strain} is a $[N,3,3]$ matrix product and fuses
  trivially; \code{grid\_update} is $O(\text{cells})$ and is a different kernel shape. Porting them
  also settles \S5.4: capture exists to paper over exactly these two, so its $1.73\times$ should be
  re-measured once they are gone.
\item \textbf{The atomics are the wall.} \code{warp} sits at $\approx$8--10\% of peak on both cards
  and does not improve when the card gets faster, which is the same signature \code{default} showed
  for a different reason. The fix is the node-centric P2G that NVIDIA's own \code{warp.fem} MPM uses:
  sort particles by cell into CSR, one thread \emph{per grid node}, each summing its own
  contributions with a plain \code{+=} --- zero atomics. The read amplification that argument was
  rejected on earlier ($27\times$) is a coalesced streaming read, not the same kind of cost as a
  serialised atomic write.
\item \textbf{Memory is no longer the constraint, and that is new.} At 0.309\,GiB per million
  particles an 80\,GiB A100 holds $\approx$250\,M and a 48\,GiB A6000 $\approx$150\,M. Past
  $\approx$20\,M the fixed $96^3$ grid of this benchmark stops being a discretisation anyone would
  choose, so the next question is not how many particles fit but what grid they need --- and a finer
  grid costs substeps through the CFL bound $\Delta t \le \mathrm{cfl}\cdot\Delta x/c$, which is a
  different scaling problem from this one.
\end{enumerate}
