\section{What was asked, and what it turned into}

The request was to simulate an eyeball: four muscles to aim it, a skull to keep it in its
socket, two more muscles for torsion, and a multi-panel movie showing deformation, stress and
activation. It grew two constraints along the way that changed the model completely.

\textbf{The eye should deform a little.} Not a rigid body on a ball joint --- a soft globe with
a stiff scleral shell, so muscle tension actually dimples the sclera and the strain runs into
the interior. That is what makes MPM the right tool rather than an unnecessary one.

\textbf{The muscles should be made of particles too, and be seen contracting.} This is the
constraint that mattered. In the first version a muscle was a line of action and its pull was a
body force injected at the insertion patch. Nothing contracted, nothing deformed, and the
tendon's grip on the sclera was \emph{asserted}. In the version reported here the muscles are
tissue --- their own set of material points --- and nothing applies a force ``to the eye'':

\begin{quote}
a muscle shortens because an active stress acts along its fibres; it cannot reel itself up
because its origin end is anchored to bone; and it moves the eye because its tendon end is
embedded in the sclera and the two bodies share one MLS-MPM background grid.
\end{quote}

The globe rotates because a muscle got shorter. That is the whole mechanism.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=grey!6,colframe=grey!45,boxrule=0.7pt,arc=2pt]
\textbf{Nothing was promoted.} All nine new operators live in \code{prototype/eye/eye\_ops.py}
and \code{prototype/eye/muscle\_ops.py}; importing them registers them, and the run is executed
by the stock \code{plexus.schema.load} and \code{plexus.engine.run}. \code{src/plexus} is
untouched. The deliverable is a \code{spec.yaml}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\midrule

\code{muscle} & the six extraocular muscles & \code{act} (innervation, the only integrated block), \code{tension}, \code{length} \\
\code{muscle\_particle} & each muscle's material points (parent \code{muscle}) & as \code{mpm\_particle} \\
\code{orbit} & the bony socket & \code{pos}, the centre of the cup \\
\code{mpm\_grid} (field) & \textbf{one} background grid, shared & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The shared grid is the point of the design: it is what mechanically couples a contracting
muscle to the sclera, and it is free --- sharing a background grid is simply how MLS-MPM couples
any two bodies. The stock \code{mpm\_scatter} \emph{overwrites} the grid, so a second
\emph{implementation} of that same contract, \code{implementation: accumulate}, was registered:
identical biology, different numerics. Per Plexus\,2 that is the correct way to extend --- a new
implementation of an existing contract, never an edit to the engine.

\section{The one result worth defending}

\textbf{No muscle action is tabulated anywhere in this prototype.} Each muscle's rotation axis is
re-measured every frame from where its tissue actually is, as $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf
u}$ (insertion radius $\times$ line of action), and \code{oculomotor\_drive} simply rectifies the
projection of the desired rotation onto it --- Sherrington's law of reciprocal innervation, one
line of code. So the textbook primary/secondary/tertiary actions have to \emph{emerge} from where
each muscle inserts and where it pulls from. They do:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{muscle} & \textbf{measured axis $(x,y,z)$} & \textbf{reading} & \textbf{textbook} \\
\midrule
LR & $(\,0.03,\ \ 1.00,\ -0.05)$ & pure abduction & abduction only \\
MR & $(\,0.02,\ -1.00,\ \ 0.03)$ & pure adduction & adduction only \\
SR & $(-0.90,\ -0.17,\ \ 0.39)$ & elevation $>$ intorsion $>$ adduction & same order \\
IR & $(\,0.90,\ -0.22,\ -0.37)$ & depression $>$ extorsion $>$ adduction & same order \\
SO & $(\,0.61,\ \ 0.14,\ \ 0.78)$ & intorsion $>$ depression $>$ abduction & same order \\
IO & $(-0.66,\ \ 0.18,\ -0.73)$ & extorsion $>$ elevation $>$ abduction & same order \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All six agree, in sign and in rank. The medial tilt of the orbital apex is what gives the
vertical recti their torsional and horizontal components; a co-axial apex makes SR and IR pure
elevators and depressors, which is anatomically wrong. Recruitment measured from the runs agrees
with this table on 4 of 4 commands in every mechanically-stable trial.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=good!5,colframe=good!45,boxrule=0.7pt,arc=2pt]
\textbf{Why this is worth more than a nice movie.} It is a falsifiable prediction that the
geometry alone determines the actions, tested against a table nobody consulted while writing the
code. It is also the Plexus point in miniature: the mechanism is in the operators and the
anatomy, not in a lookup table inside a simulator.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Two modelling decisions that are not obvious}

\textbf{The globe is an ovoid; the cup is a sphere.} Teleost eyes are flattened along the optic
axis. The \code{mpm\_particle} entity can only seed a uniform ball, so the rest configuration is
that ball affinely squashed, $z\to kz$ with $k=0.82$. An affine image of a uniform ball is a
uniform ellipsoid, so density stays uniform, and because this is an \emph{initial condition} and
not a deformation, $F$ stays the identity --- the ovoid is undeformed and carries no residual
stress. The bony cup, by contrast, stays spherical with a radius just above the equatorial
semi-axis: an ovoid cannot rotate inside a socket that matches it, and in the animal the gap is
filled by orbital fat, not bone.

\textbf{The fat exerts no torque.} The retrobulbar fat pad is modelled as a restoring force
toward the socket centre applied \emph{uniformly} to every point of the globe. A uniform body
force produces no torque about the centroid, so the fat recentres the eye without ever resisting
gaze --- which is exactly the division of labour the anatomy has. Without it, the residual normal
component of muscle tension slowly walks the globe around inside the cup.

\section{The four things that went wrong, and what each one taught}

Eleven archived trials, each with its own \code{spec.yaml}, \code{movie.mp4} and \code{diag.json}.
The interesting content is the failures.

\begin{phasebox}{1. Raising active stress alone (t02) --- the muscles ate themselves}
At $A/E = 0.8$ the straps shortened by 88\% and crushed the globe (strain $\gg 1$). A muscle
shortens until its passive tension balances its active stress, so the \emph{steady shortening is
set by $A/E$ alone}, while the \emph{force} it delivers is $A\times$cross-section. Raising $A$
without raising $E$ only buys collapse. \textbf{Lesson: $A$ and $E$ are not independent knobs.}
\end{phasebox}

\begin{phasebox}{2. Coupling them (t03--t04) --- a buckling cliff, and a new ceiling}
Held at $A/E=0.25$ everything is well behaved: 20--27\% shortening, 4/4 correct recruitment,
2--5\% strain, negligible drift. At $A/E=0.30$ it collapses again --- so the limit is a
\emph{buckling} cliff, not a smooth trade-off. But the well-behaved regime capped the eye at
$11^\circ$. \textbf{Lesson: a stiff passive element buys stability by making the antagonist so
stiff that it eats most of the agonist's force.}
\end{phasebox}

\begin{phasebox}{3. Geometry (t06--t08) --- length is what buys gaze}
For a muscle pulling a globe against its antagonist, $\theta_{\max}\sim (A/E)\times(L/R)$. An
earlier convenience --- truncating each muscle to 55\% of its path so the four recti would not
project onto one blob --- had left the recti at $L/R=2.3$ and the obliques at 1.15, against
$\approx 3.3$ in a real orbit. Restoring the length, spreading the rectus origins around the
\emph{annulus of Zinn} (a ring, not a point) and widening the sclera stand-off took horizontal
range from $12.7^\circ$ to $29.6^\circ$ with recruitment still 4/4. \textbf{Lesson: the
convenience taken for the picture was also a mechanical error.}
\end{phasebox}

\begin{phasebox}{4. The force--length relation (t09--t16) --- the passive element was backwards}
Real muscle is passively \emph{soft} and limits its own shortening through the force--length
relation, not through a stiff passive element. Switching on
$T = A\,a\,(1+\beta(\lambda-1))$ means a muscle stops pulling once it has shortened by $1/\beta$,
so $E$ can drop far below $A$ and the antagonist gets out of the way: horizontal range jumped to
$52^\circ$ and torsion to $13.7^\circ$. But a soft, slender strap then \emph{buckles} --- it folds
up, losing 60\% of its centreline length while the fibre stretch stays near $0.75$, so the
limiter never engages. The fixes are what the real plant does: a chunkier cross-section (bending
stiffness goes as $wh^3$) and genuine tonic co-contraction, which keeps every muscle in tension
and is precisely why extraocular motoneurons fire at high rates even in primary position.
\textbf{Lesson: two different instabilities, and only one of them was the one being fixed.}
\end{phasebox}

\begin{phasebox}{5. The orbital pulley (t15--t22) --- the mechanism that was actually missing}
A free-floating strap is a column in compression the moment it contracts, and the fix is not
numerical: every extraocular muscle passes through a sleeve of collagen and smooth muscle fixed
to the orbital wall (Demer's active-pulley hypothesis), and that sleeve is exactly a
\emph{transverse} constraint --- it fixes where the path runs without resisting how much the
muscle shortens. \code{muscle\_sleeve} applies a restoring force to only the component of
displacement perpendicular to the local fibre, tapering to zero over the distal third so the
tendon end stays free to follow the rotating globe. Held at everything else constant, it raises
the $A/E$ buckling ceiling from $\approx0.25$ to between $0.28$ and $0.38$ --- $A/E=0.28$ runs
clean (20--30\% shortening, finite, strain $0.051$), $A/E=0.38$ still folds.
\end{phasebox}

\begin{phasebox}{6. The globe came apart (t18) --- absolute stiffness, not just contrast}
Under the strongest drives the globe stopped being a globe. The vitreous had been set at
$E=9$, a shear modulus of $3.8$ --- very nearly a fluid. The fixed-corotated law has no failure
criterion, so under a hard tendon pull the interior simply flowed and the shell separated from
it. Raising the interior to $E=45$ (vitreous) and $130$ (choroid) against a $420$ sclera keeps
the $\approx 9\times$ core-to-shell contrast the strain panel is there to show, while giving
every region enough shear modulus to hold together. \textbf{Lesson: ``deformable'' is a
statement about the ratio; ``stays in one piece'' is a statement about the absolute value.}
\end{phasebox}

\section{Gradient descent on the controller --- and why it was refused}

\code{tune\_gaze.py} follows \code{inverse\_slime}: differentiating the MPM rollout is exactly
the cost Plexus\,2 warns about (memory scales as points $\times$ steps), so it never rolls out.
It identifies a reduced plant --- $\ddot\theta = Bu - C\dot\theta - K\theta$ per axis, with
$u=\sum_m a_m \hat{\mathbf a}_m$ read straight from the archived run --- by one ridge
least-squares fit, then runs Adam on $(k_p,k_i,k_d,\text{gain},\text{tonic})$ over that
differentiable surrogate.

It works mechanically, and the answer is negative, twice over.

\textbf{First the derivatives were the problem, not the model.} The initial fit gave
$R^2 = 0.28$--$0.40$ on $\ddot\theta$. Differentiating a strided, noisy signal twice amplifies
its noise by $\sim h^{-2}$; reading the derivatives off a local Savitzky--Golay polynomial
instead raised it to $0.48$--$0.72$ with no change to the model.

\textbf{Then the model was the problem.} Tuning still bought $1.008\times$. The first
explanation offered --- ``the drive must be saturated'' --- was wrong, and the tool's own
diagnostic said so: the agonist sits above $0.98$ on only 6\% of frames. So the surrogate was
asked to reproduce the excursion the real eye actually made, under the gains it actually used:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
peak excursion & horizontal & vertical & torsion \\
\midrule
real            & $17.0^\circ$ & $9.0^\circ$ & $2.8^\circ$ \\
surrogate       & $\ \ 1.7^\circ$ & $3.0^\circ$ & $2.2^\circ$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bad!4,colframe=bad!45,boxrule=0.7pt,arc=2pt]
\textbf{The instrument failed its own check, so the measurement was thrown away.} A good $R^2$
on $\ddot\theta$ certifies the \emph{transients}; the standing error we wanted to tune away
lives in the steady state $\theta_{ss}=Bu/K$, and the fit misses the real excursion by a factor
of ten in the horizontal. Tuning gains on it would be optimising against a model that disagrees
with the thing it is a model of. \code{tune\_gaze} now runs this fidelity test and refuses to
report a tuned number when it fails --- which is the discipline the Plexus\,2 paper puts first:
verify the instrument before trusting the measurement.
\end{tcolorbox}

The fix is known and not yet done: excite the plant with a frequency sweep rather than a
staircase, and fit the steady state explicitly instead of hoping it falls out of an
acceleration fit.

\section{Where it stands, and what is next}

\textbf{Settled.} The decomposition into sets; the emergent muscle-action table; the two-body
grid coupling via a new \emph{implementation} of an existing contract; the deformable ovoid in a
spherical cup with a torque-free fat suspension; the orbital pulley and the $A/E$ ceiling it
raises ($0.28$ clean, $0.32$ folds); the globe radius as a metric, an acceptance check and a
term in the objective, so ``it hit the angle'' can no longer be bought by crushing the eye; and
the archive discipline --- twenty-five trials, each keeping its own spec, movie and numbers.

\textbf{Open.} Reaching the commanded angle rather than about 60\% of it. Six rounds each found a real mechanism and a real ceiling, and the
configuration that survives all of them (t21) is stable, holds together, contracts realistically
and tracks three times better than anything before it --- but it still settles at roughly 60\% of
a $25^\circ$ command. The remaining authority has to come from the pulley ceiling, which is known
to lie between $A/E=0.28$ and $0.38$ and has not been bisected. The acceptance test is written and
automatic: reaches its commands within $6^\circ$, torsion above $6^\circ$, horizontal above
$35^\circ$, recruitment 4/4, centroid drift below 6\% of the globe radius, globe strain between
0.4\% and 12\%, and peak shortening between 4\% and 38\%.

\textbf{Worth doing next, in order.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Finish the working point, then render the long \code{atlas} program (each antagonist pair
driven back and forth three times) and the zebrafish \code{okr} program at movie resolution.
\item Let the video-captioning VLM watch the result cold and see whether it reports a rolling
eye. That is the dynamics-watching critic the Plexus\,2 paper names as a required agent, and
this is a cheap, honest test of it.
\item Repair the surrogate identification (\S6): sweep excitation, explicit steady-state fit.
Only then is controller tuning a question worth asking.
\item A real inverse pass: given only the observed gaze trace, recover the six insertion
geometries. Loop~II on a system whose ground truth we wrote down ourselves.
\end{enumerate}
