\title{\textbf{Discovering Developmental Mechanisms by Operator Composition:\\[2pt]
An Active-Matter $\times$ MPM Embryo in Plexus}}
\author{Plexus prototype \texttt{prototype/embryogenesis}}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Embryonic development emerges from the interplay of mechanics, active migration, proliferation and
biochemical signalling across multiple spatial and temporal scales. Rather than prescribing these
behaviours, we ask whether they can \emph{emerge} from a small library of reusable physical operators
composed within a common differentiable simulation framework. This reframes modelling: instead of
fitting parameters inside a fixed simulator, we treat the operator set itself as the object of
discovery, searching over \emph{mechanisms}---their presence, absence, and composition---with parameter
tuning only after a candidate mechanism exists. The scientific objective is to identify the minimal
combinations of operators that generate successive developmental phenomena, which requires a
compositional substrate in which mechanisms can be systematically recombined while sharing a common
physics. Using Plexus, we progressively construct an active-matter $\times$ MPM embryo in which each
developmental capability is established and quantitatively validated in isolation before being
integrated into a complete system. Organized as a staged mechanistic program---each stage answering one
biological question before the next---this strategy transforms embryogenesis from a
parameter-optimization problem into a causal investigation of developmental mechanisms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Developmental biology is inherently multi-mechanistic: shape, motion, pattern and growth arise together
from mechanics, active cell motility, proliferation and diffusible signalling. Most computational models
of morphogenesis begin by \emph{fixing} the governing mechanisms---a purpose-built vertex model, a
reaction--diffusion system, a tissue-mechanics solver---and then calibrate parameters to match an
observation. The mechanisms are assumptions; only their coefficients are inferred.

We take the opposite stance and regard the \emph{set of mechanisms} as the primary unknown. The search
space is over operators (Does confinement help or hurt? Is division a growth mechanism or a mixing one?
Which coupling actually deforms the tissue?), and parameter tuning is a secondary refinement applied
only \emph{after} a candidate mechanism has been shown to matter. This is a substantially different
modelling philosophy, and it requires a substrate that makes mechanisms first-class, interchangeable
objects rather than hard-wired assumptions.

Plexus is such a substrate: a compositional, differentiable simulation framework in which every
biological process is a \emph{reusable operator} acting on shared physical state, and simulations are
assembled by composing operators rather than by writing a bespoke solver. Here, self-propelled
active-matter \emph{agents} (cells) are embedded in a Material Point Method (MPM) \emph{continuum} (the
embryonic tissue), and the two subsystems communicate through a shared background grid. Because
operators can be added, removed and recombined while sharing one physics, the same framework lets us ask
causal questions---introduce one operator, ablate another, and measure the effect on a common set of
observables. A staged, hypothesis-driven exploration then walks the embryo up a ladder of developmental
capabilities, each established before the next is attempted. Plexus is therefore not merely a simulator
for this problem but the medium in which the mechanistic search itself is expressed.

\section{Methods}
All experiments are performed in Plexus. Cells are modelled as active agents embedded within a continuum
(MLS-)MPM representation of the embryo. Agent operators govern migration, chemotaxis, proliferation and
growth; continuum operators govern elasticity, momentum transfer and large deformation. Rather than
implementing a dedicated embryo simulator, developmental behaviours are obtained by composing generic
operators---chemotaxis, active migration, division, growth, and agent--continuum coupling---so that no
mechanism is hard-wired into the solver.

The investigation follows a strictly staged protocol. Each batch tests a single mechanistic hypothesis
and advances only after the previous developmental capability has been \emph{quantitatively} established
against a fixed scorecard (shape, organization, flow, partition, coupling) and gated by hard-failure
constraints (no collapse, no escape, exclusion respected, balance-bounded acceleration). This avoids
confounding later phenomena with failures of earlier prerequisites and produces a cumulative map of
causal relationships between operators and emergent developmental behaviour.

\section{Results}
The program is a ladder: a stable blastula, membrane deformation, division, collective flow, partition,
integration, orientation, growth, patterning, and morphogenesis. We summarize each in turn.

\paragraph{Stable blastula.}
The first objective was to determine whether a mechanically stable blastula could exist without
pathological collapse or cell escape. By progressively separating the effects of confinement, repulsion
and density, a reproducible operating point was established in which cells remained evenly distributed
while continuously interacting with the surrounding continuum (Fig.~\ref{fig:blastula}). Over-confinement
crushed cells together (collapse); removing it sprayed them out of the core (escape). A narrow low-press
window with strong short-range exclusion held both hard-failures at bay.

\paragraph{Membrane deformation.}
Once stability was achieved, we asked whether internal cellular activity could mechanically deform the
surrounding tissue. Systematic exploration showed that deformation is generated primarily through the
agent--continuum coupling (momentum scattered from cells into the material) rather than by membrane
softening or rotational forcing. The resulting shell deformation established the first mechanically
active blastula while preserving stability.

\paragraph{Division is mixing, not growth.}
We next asked whether proliferation alone could generate developmental growth. Division robustly
increased population size and deformed the embryo, but repeated experiments demonstrated that
proliferation fundamentally behaves as a mechanical \emph{mixing} process: increasing division
progressively destroyed previously established spatial organization. Division is therefore not a true
developmental growth mechanism---a conclusion that later motivates a dedicated growth primitive.

\paragraph{Collective flow.}
Attention then shifted to collective motion. Several first-order alignment mechanisms and
continuum-mediated feedbacks were explored. While coherent migration could be generated transiently,
sustained collective flow proved fundamentally limited by a trade-off between coherence and mechanical
containment. The integrated operating point nevertheless demonstrated the coexistence of continuous
motion, bounded proliferation and mechanical deformation.

\paragraph{Partition.}
Having established a mechanically active blastula, we addressed tissue partitioning. Differential
self-adhesion produced only weak and irreproducible segregation. In contrast, heterotypic two-channel
chemotactic cross-repulsion generated robust lateral demixing that replicated across seeds and
established the first stable multicellular pattern within the embryo (Fig.~\ref{fig:partition}).

\paragraph{Integration.}
The integration stage asked whether deformation, proliferation, flow and partition could coexist within
a single embryo. A compatible operating regime was identified, demonstrating a flowing, dividing and
self-partitioning blastula. The principal limitation revealed here was a hard trade-off between
increasing proliferation and maintaining organization, with division again acting as a mechanical source
of mixing.

\paragraph{Programmable orientation.}
The next stage introduced embryonic polarity. Uniform external forces oriented only the global body,
whereas \emph{differential} sedimentation between cell types generated a reproducible animal--vegetal
axis. Changing the direction of the imposed differential drift continuously steered the axis,
establishing a controllable developmental polarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:orientation}).

\paragraph{Continuous growth as a new primitive.}
These results exposed a conceptual limitation of proliferation-based development: increasing cell number
alone cannot produce genuine tissue expansion. We therefore introduced a new developmental primitive,
\op{cell\_grow}, which performs continuous rest-volume growth of the \emph{cell} independently of
division; particle addition is merely its numerical realization. An initial density-only implementation
failed to expand the envelope; inserting \emph{prestretched} reserve material---woken particles that
carry outward pressure and relax by pushing their neighbours apart---converted growth into genuine
volumetric expansion. This established continuous growth as a biological mechanism distinct from
proliferation.

\paragraph{Growth preserves pattern.}
With continuous growth available, we asked whether spatial patterning survives tissue expansion. Unlike
proliferation, which mechanically remixes neighbours, continuous growth preserved the previously
established chemotactic domains; differential sedimentation alone then sufficed to orient and complete
the growing pattern, showing that growth and pattern formation are compatible (Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}).

\paragraph{Morphogenesis.}
The final stage explored morphogenesis through anisotropic growth. Directionally biased growth produced
reproducible body buds while maintaining the molecular pattern (Fig.~\ref{fig:morphogenesis}).
Unexpectedly, elongation \emph{parallel} to the developmental polarity destabilized the partition,
whereas growth \emph{perpendicular} to the polarity yielded the most stable body geometry---suggesting
that morphology and molecular organization interact geometrically, with elongation preferentially
orthogonal to the polarity axis.

\section{Conclusion}
The complete developmental program identifies a hierarchy of compatible mechanisms: stable blastula
formation enabled mechanical deformation, which enabled reproducible partitioning, which enabled
programmable orientation, which enabled continuous growth, which in turn enabled morphogenesis. The only
persistently incompatible component was sustained proliferation, which consistently acted as a mechanical
mixing process that limits organization. Rather than producing a single optimized embryo, the study
delivers a \emph{causal map} relating individual physical operators to successive stages of embryonic
development---and, more broadly, demonstrates a modelling philosophy in which the mechanisms themselves,
not merely their parameters, are the object of discovery.
