Plexus

We introduce Plexus, a typed intermediate representation for biological mechanisms. Rather than defining yet another simulator, Plexus defines a common language whose primary objects are biological operators. Every operator specifies the biological entities it acts upon, the states it requires, the relations between these entities, and one or more numerical implementations. The Plexus research program has three objectives. First, to define an algebra of biological operators. Second, to decompose the existing corpus of simulation models into this common language. Third, to construct differentiable implementations of these operators so that mechanistic models can be directly inferred from biological data.

Just as compilers separate programming languages from their implementations through an intermediate representation, Plexus separates biological mechanisms from their numerical implementations. Every operator is defined by a typed signature — what biological transformation it performs — and one or more implementations — how it is numerically realized. The signature is the biological contract; the implementation is a numerical choice.

Every operator may admit multiple implementations satisfying the same signature: a mechanical interaction by Material Point Methods, finite elements, vertex or phase-field models; a signalling operator by Hodgkin–Huxley equations, Neural ODEs or graph neural networks. Because a mechanism is stated once, independently of its solver, existing simulation frameworks become complementary backends implementing a common intermediate representation rather than competing modelling paradigms.

Four concepts, seven operators

The language is built around four fundamental concepts: operators, sets (collections of biological entities across scales — particles, cells, neurons, synapses, tissues), the states each element carries, and fields (continuous quantities defined over space — morphogens, pressure, potentials). A biological model is a composition of operators acting on these objects.

The central hypothesis of Plexus is that a large fraction of existing biological simulation models can be expressed as compositions of a small number of elementary operator families. The proposed algebra consists of seven:

  • Lateral — interactions within a biological set.
  • Aggregate ↑ and Broadcast ↓ — many-to-one and one-to-many transformations across biological scales.
  • Exchange — transformations between sets and fields.
  • Rewire — modification of relations between biological entities.
  • Divide and Die — creation and removal of biological entities.

The first four families transform biological state while preserving the underlying organization; Rewire modifies the interaction graph; Divide and Die modify the entities themselves. The full algebra — with typed signatures and the reference implementation — is on The language.

Composing mechanisms

Biological models are expressed as compositions of elementary operators. Plexus represents a model as an ordered sequence of operator applications — a schedule — specifying both the order and the frequency with which operators are evaluated. Formally, one simulation step is the composition \(\Phi = \mathcal{O}_n \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{O}_1\), whose repeated application generates the system dynamics \(x_{t+1}=\Phi(x_t)\). Different operators may evolve on different time scales — mechanical interactions every step, diffusion, growth or division less often — so a model is a multi-rate composition of biological mechanisms rather than a monolithic solver.

Operators are pure transformations: rather than modifying state in place, each returns its contribution \(\Delta_i\) to the system evolution, and the complete update \(\Delta = \sum_i \Delta_i\) is passed to the numerical implementation. Introducing a new mechanism is therefore adding a new operator to the schedule, never editing the engine — and because nothing is mutated in place, gradients propagate through the composition and the whole rollout stays differentiable.

What it looks like

Qualitatively different dynamical systems, all expressed in the same operator language. Each clip below is a single spec.yaml over the identical operators and schedule grammar — hover (or tab to) a title to read the exact spec that generated it.

Interaction & flocking

Lateral operators over a radius neighbour graph: a per-type force law (attraction–repulsion) and the three boids rules that sum to flocking.

Reference — attraction–repulsion and boids adapted from ParticleGraph (Allier et al.); the Vicsek model after Vicsek et al. (1995).

Turing — Gray–Scott reaction–diffusion

Reaction–diffusion on the cell–cell adjacency graph (graph_diffuse → react) — Turing's two-species activator–inhibitor system, on a 2D cell disc: the coral regime, a fine maze (small χ), and a diverging activator view (high → red, low → blue, balanced → white).

Reference — Gray–Scott and Brusselator reaction–diffusion, after their originating models.

Epithelial mechanics — a true vertex model

An explicit vertex model, via Tyssue. Unlike a self-propelled Voronoi tissue (each cell a single point), here cells are polygons that share junction vertices, and those junction vertices — not the cell centres — are the mechanical degrees of freedom. An area + line-tension shape energy (shape_energy) relaxes the mesh toward force balance, with growth, division and cell death as explicit operations on the mesh.

Reference — the Tyssue vertex-model library; active vertex model after Farhadifar et al. (2007).

Cell death as a sculptor, not as cleanup. Death is one of the eight elementary operator families, and it was the only one this vesicle lacked: growth inflates and division subdivides, so every mechanism it owned pushed the surface outward. A dying cell shrinks, sheds its neighbours through T1 exchanges until it is a triangle, and is then extruded by a T2 collapse — the sheet closing over the gap by force balance alone. Cells marked to die are drawn blue. Removing 71 % of the cells leaves the surface closed, Euler 2, with no broken face.

Reference — apoptotic force driving epithelial folding after Monier et al., Nature 518:245 (2015).

Vertex + Turing — patterning & shaping a growing tissue

Growth and reaction–diffusion on a deformable vertex shell: the tissue first proliferates by growth and division to a couple of thousand cells, then reaction–diffusion chemistry both colours the surface and drives its shape — each regime buckling the shell a different way.

Reference — growth on the Tyssue vertex model; reaction–diffusion coupled to a vertex-model vesicle (our Turing × vertex prototype).

Two morphogens in competition. The same shell, carrying a second Gray–Scott system that lives on the same cells and reads none of the first one's numbers: species A (red, fine) is what grow_3d and divide_3d listen to, and species B — twice the diffusivities, so a coarser wavelength — is there to oppose it. There are two ways to oppose, and the first two clips are one each. As an inhibitor, B switches growth off where it peaks, and the cell is drawn teal: that is the half every growth law here was missing, because rate × (ρ + hill(a)) is activating-only and its floor ρ is never zero, so the tissue between the spots keeps inflating no matter what — an inhibitor is what supplies the zero. As a death signal, B marks the cell for apoptosis where it runs LOW, drawn blue; the mark is the only visible part, because a marked cell then shrinks at a fixed rate, is removed, and its neighbours close the gap by a T1 exchange. The third clip is the control that says why either needs a SECOND field: with one, the opposing rule can only read the very chemical that drives growth, so it lands in the antiphase of the growth pattern and nowhere else.

Vertex + MPM

Two Levels, two solvers, one interface: a triangulated surface with mass and springs — the vertex model's own geometry — pressed into a block of MPM material (the MLS-MPM cycle below), coupled particle-to-face so the reaction really returns to the mesh rather than being applied to the material alone. No tissue and no matrix here: this is the interface itself, on the smallest rig that can falsify it, under the three loadings that can break it. Press: the surface indents the block against a rigid floor and lifts off again, and the material has to push back and recover. Drag: a sticky pad presses to a fixed depth and then travels sideways over a block pinned at its base, so the load is tangential — it ploughs, heaps material ahead of itself and shears what it drags over, coloured by von Mises because a drag changes shape at constant volume and a volumetric colour is blank exactly where the physics is. Breach: the surface carries a hole and the box is closed on all four sides, so the material has nowhere to go but through, and it extrudes as a plug. Each clip plays the 3D view first, then a cross-section through the same instant; the grey lines are the rigid floor and, in the last one, the walls.

Reference — the contact is the ICFEMP particle-to-surface scheme of Chen, Qiu, Zhang & Lian (2015), Comput. Methods Appl. Mech. Engrg. 293:1–19, chosen over grid-node coupling (Lian, Zhang & Liu 2011, CMAME 200:3482) because that one needs the mesh and the grid to be comparable in size and ours are not; the solid is MLS-MPM (Hu et al. 2018), the surface is the mesh of the 3D active vertex model of Okuda et al. (2018). The implementation is ours (prototype/ecm): these three clips are the interface rig test_03_mesh_contact, and the operator that carries the same scheme onto a curved, dividing surface is mesh_contact, with seed_ecm / cell_to_ecm / cell_exclude_3d / ecm_stress beside it.

Continuum mechanics — MLS-MPM

The same particle Level, now a material: the decomposed MLS-MPM cycle (strain → scatter → grid solve → gather) carries elastic, fluid, and granular bodies. Each clip plays the default material render; most also show the run coloured by deformation (‖F‖), then stress.

Reference — the MLS-MPM solver is our PyTorch port (saalfeldlab/MPM_pytorch) of Yuanming Hu’s taichi_mpm, implementing MLS-MPM (Hu et al. 2018).

Stigmergy — Physarum slime (one species, a disc colony)

A set of agents coupled to a scalar field: agents deposit a trail, the field diffuses and decays, agents sense and steer up it — a self-reinforcing transport network (one schedule: deposit → diffuse → decay → sense → steer). Here a single species is seeded in a disc (spawn: disc), so the colony stays a bounded circular web; the three differ only in turn rate and sensor geometry.

Reference — the Physarum agent is adapted from Sebastian Lague’s Slime-Simulation.

Stigmergy — Physarum slime (many species, space-filling)

The same schedule, but agents are now seeded uniformly across the field (spawn: random) and split into 4–8 species, each depositing and sensing its own trail channel with cross-species inhibition (cross: −1). That is why this row looks so unlike the discs above: instead of one bounded colony, the networks fill the whole plane and segregate into distinct interwoven colours.

Reference — the Physarum agent is adapted from Sebastian Lague’s Slime-Simulation.

Strange attractors

A single lateral operator rides a whole cloud along a dissipative 3D chaotic flow ẋ = f(x); a tiny seed is stretched-and-folded until the cloud is the attractor. Ten systems ship; six shown.

Reference — each system cites its originating paper (Lorenz 1963, Rössler 1976, Sprott 1994, …).

Inverse-square law — self-gravity & electrostatics

One squared_law operator, two regimes: law: gravity (mass, always attract, all-pairs) collapses a disc into a galaxy; law: coulomb (signed charge, like-repel) is a plasma.

Reference — softened self-gravity after Philip Mocz’s Create Your Own N-body Simulation (2020); Coulomb after ParticleGraph.

Agentic mechanistic discovery

The explicit representation of biological mechanisms transforms scientific discovery from a parameter optimization problem into a language exploration problem. Because mechanisms are explicit operators rather than simulator-specific code, they are directly accessible to agentic systems. Plexus organizes this as three loops, each at a different level of abstraction:

  • Loop I — Understanding. Decompose an existing simulator into Plexus operators, recovering an explicit mechanistic description from opaque code.
  • Loop II — Fitting. Fit the resulting composition to observations through its differentiable implementations, preserving the link between parameters and mechanisms.
  • Loop III — Discovery. Read the residual: propose new operators, compare competing mechanistic hypotheses, and extend the algebra when existing mechanisms fall short.

The language itself becomes the object of discovery — new biological knowledge is a new operator, not merely a tuned parameter.

The operator atlas

This defines a research program with three objectives: build an algebra of biological operators; decompose the existing corpus of simulation frameworks into that common language; and construct differentiable implementations so mechanistic models can be inferred from data. The second objective is the operator atlas — existing frameworks (Chaste, CompuCell3D, Morpheus, Tissue Forge, PhysiCell, Jaxley, BrainPy, connectome GNNs, …) treated not as rival simulators but as repositories of reusable mechanisms, each decomposed into operators, typed signatures, and implementations.

The atlas is also a direct test of the central hypothesis: if decomposing a broad collection of simulators converges toward a compact, reusable vocabulary, the operator algebra is a meaningful intermediate representation for biology; if genuinely new families keep appearing, the language remains incomplete and must be extended. The atlas therefore serves both as a repository of biological knowledge and as the mechanism through which the language itself evolves.
