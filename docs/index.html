Plexus

We introduce Plexus, a typed intermediate representation for biological mechanisms. Rather than defining yet another simulator, Plexus defines a common language whose primary objects are biological operators. Every operator specifies the biological entities it acts upon, the states it requires, the relations between these entities, and one or more numerical implementations. The Plexus research program has three objectives. First, to define an algebra of biological operators. Second, to decompose the existing corpus of simulation models into this common language. Third, to construct differentiable implementations of these operators so that mechanistic models can be directly inferred from biological data.

Biological systems must be decomposed not only into operators, but also into the entities on which those operators act. These entities are heterogeneous — receptors, signalling proteins, cells, extracellular matrices, tissues, fields — and are organized across biological levels. Plexus makes this organization explicit as a compositional hierarchy: each level carries its own entities and states, operators act between entities within a level, and Aggregate ↑ and Broadcast ↓ connect levels by mapping state upward and downward. Decomposition therefore does not flatten biology into a single collection of variables; it preserves the organization through which mechanisms at one level are composed with mechanisms at another.

The purpose of this decomposition is ultimately recomposition: to construct complete mechanistic models from reusable entities and operators. Making such compositions executable requires strong structural choices. Plexus separates the language that specifies the biological model from the code that interprets and executes it. The language declares entities, states, relations, hierarchy and operators; the interpreter resolves this description into concrete numerical implementations and schedules their execution.

The language

Plexus defines a typed language for expressing biological mechanisms independently of their numerical realization. The language is built around four fundamental concepts: operators, sets, states, and fields. Biological models are therefore represented as compositions of operators acting on discrete and continuous biological objects rather than simulator-specific algorithms.

A set is a collection of biological entities at one scale — proteins, cells, neurons, synapses, tissues; the state is what each element of a set carries; a field is a continuous quantity defined over space — a morphogen, a pressure, a potential.

The central hypothesis of Plexus is that a large fraction of existing biological simulation models can be expressed as compositions of a small number of elementary operator kinds. The proposed algebra consists of eight:

  • Lateral — interactions within a biological set.
  • Aggregate ↑ and Broadcast ↓ — many-to-one and one-to-many transformations across biological scales.
  • Exchange — transformations between sets and fields.
  • Field — a field’s own dynamics: diffusion, decay, reaction.
  • Rewire — modification of relations between biological entities.
  • Structural — creation and removal of biological entities: Divide and Die.
  • Seed — the initial state, written once and never again.

The first four kinds transform the state of a set and Field that of a field, all while preserving the underlying organization; Rewire modifies the interaction graph; Structural modifies the entities themselves; Seed writes what the others evolve. Details in Language.

Composing mechanisms

Biological models are expressed as compositions of elementary operators. Plexus represents a model as an ordered sequence of operator applications — a schedule — specifying both the order and the frequency with which operators are evaluated. Formally, one simulation step is the composition \(\Phi = \mathcal{O}_n \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{O}_1\), whose repeated application generates the system dynamics \(x_{t+1}=\Phi(x_t)\). Different operators may evolve on different time scales — mechanical interactions every step, diffusion, growth or division less often — so a model is a multi-rate composition of biological mechanisms rather than a monolithic solver.

Operators are pure transformations: rather than modifying state in place, each returns its contribution \(\Delta_i\) to the system evolution, and the complete update \(\Delta = \sum_i \Delta_i\) is passed to the numerical implementation. Introducing a new mechanism is therefore adding a new operator to the schedule, never editing the engine — and because nothing is mutated in place, gradients propagate through the composition and the whole rollout stays differentiable.

Composing hierarchy

A set may be contained in another — proteins in a cell, cells in a tissue, tissues in an organism — through an explicit child → parent map. Importantly, a parent may contain several distinct sets: the same cell can contain receptors, signalling proteins, cytoskeletal elements and particles. These sets remain distinct objects, with their own state and operators, while sharing the same parent. Each level can also run at its own rate: no level is reduced to a parameter of the one above it.

Operators can act within a level or across levels. Within a cell, for example, operators may couple receptors → signalling proteins → cytoskeleton. Across the containment map, Aggregate ↑ builds parent state from children — a cell position from its particles, or a neuron input from its synapses — while Broadcast ↓ propagates parent state to children — a material property to every particle owned by a cell, or a constraint imposed by the parent.

Fields are level-specific too. Exchange couples a set to a field at the appropriate level, allowing a protein concentration field, a cellular morphogen field and a tissue mechanical grid to coexist as distinct objects.

The consequence is that proteins → cells → tissues is not a pipeline of separate simulators. It is one compositional graph: multiple sets may coexist inside each parent, operators connect them locally, and Aggregate/Broadcast connect the levels. The same operator kinds are reused throughout; adding another biological scale requires no new engine.

What it looks like

Qualitatively different dynamical systems, all expressed in the same operator language. Each clip below is a single spec.yaml over the identical operators and schedule grammar — hover (or tab to) a title to read the exact spec that generated it.

Turing — Gray–Scott reaction–diffusion

The simplest composition: cells carrying two molecular states, coupled by diffusion between neighbouring cells and by local activator–inhibitor reactions within each cell. The same biological entities participate in two mechanisms whose composition generates spatial patterning. Shown on a 2D cell disc are a striped regime, a spotted one obtained by changing a single reaction parameter, two chemistries coupled through their reaction, and three species in cyclic competition.

Reference — the Gray–Scott system of Gray & Scott (1983), Chem. Eng. Sci. 38:29–43, in the reaction–diffusion form and parameter map of Pearson (1993), Science 261:189–192; the Brusselator is Prigogine & Lefever (1968), J. Chem. Phys. 48:1695–1700.

Epithelial mechanics — a vertex model

A more structured representation of epithelial cells and their shared junctions. Cells are polygons whose boundaries share vertices, making junction geometry itself part of the biological state. Area elasticity and junction tension couple neighbouring cells and relax the tissue toward mechanical equilibrium, while growth, division and cell death modify its organization. Here the same cellular entities therefore carry geometry, interact mechanically, and change their topology over time.

Reference — active vertex model after Farhadifar et al. (2007), on the space-filling polyhedra of Honda et al. (2004), and the T1 exchanges are the reversible network reconnection of Okuda et al. (2013), Biomech. Model. Mechanobiol. 12:627–644.

Cell death — sculpting an epithelial surface

Morphogenesis emerges from the composition of mechanisms acting on the same epithelial cells. Growth expands the surface, division changes its cellular resolution, while cell death removes material and allows the tissue to contract and reshape. A dying cell progressively shrinks and loses neighbours before being extruded, while the surrounding junction mechanics close the sheet. Here, adding a single operator changes the behaviour of the composed system: removing 71% of the cells can reshape the vesicle while preserving a closed surface.

Reference — apoptotic force driving epithelial folding after Monier et al., Nature 518:245 (2015).

Growing tissue + Turing pattern

Two mechanisms now act on the same evolving tissue. Cells grow and divide, changing the geometry and adjacency on which molecular states diffuse and react; in turn, the resulting Turing pattern is carried by the growing population and shapes its surface. Neither mechanism is a post-processing step: patterning and morphogenesis evolve together on the same cells. This is a first composition of independently defined biological operators into a coupled dynamical model.

Reference — this is the experiment of Okuda et al. (2018), Sci. Rep. 8:2386 — Turing patterning on a 3D vertex model — rebuilt as Plexus operators on the Tyssue mesh; the chemistry is the Gray–Scott system cited in the Turing section above.

Continuum mechanics — MLS-MPM

Plexus can make the numerical structure of a mechanism explicit when greater physical detail is required. Here a material is represented by particles coupled through an Eulerian grid, and continuum mechanics is decomposed into strain, particle-to-grid transfer, grid dynamics and grid-to-particle transfer. The eight clips below run those same operators throughout: what changes along the row is the spec — first the constitutive law, then a single material number, then a plastic law in place of a fluid one, then one added force term; then the schedule itself gains inflow and outflow, a solid is put inside the fluid, and finally only the particle count grows, from five million to a billion. Rather than treating MLS-MPM as a monolithic simulator, Plexus exposes its material operations as composable operators.

Reference — the MLS-MPM solver is our PyTorch port (saalfeldlab/MPM_pytorch) of Yuanming Hu’s taichi_mpm, implementing MLS-MPM (Hu et al. 2018).

Vertex + MPM

The previous representations can themselves be composed. A triangulated surface and an MPM material remain distinct entities, with different states and different mechanical implementations, but interact through an explicit interface that transfers contact forces in both directions. Everything is held fixed across the four clips — the same gel, the same 500,000 material points, the same penalty contact and the same travel — so what differs between the movies is the shape of the surface, and in the last one the fact that it reaches the material by growing rather than by being pushed. This is the computational pattern needed to compose heterogeneous biological structures.

Reference — contact: ICFEMP, Chen et al. (2015), chosen over grid-node coupling (Lian et al. 2011).

Cell tissue + basement membrane + extracellular matrix

The pieces are finally recomposed into a hierarchical biological model. Cells grow and divide to form a cell tissue; the tissue deforms a distinct basement membrane; the membrane and cells interact mechanically with the surrounding extracellular matrix. At a lower level, proteases, integrins and myosins regulate these interactions. Mechanisms therefore act both within levels and across levels, while cells, membrane, matrix and molecular states retain their own representations and dynamics. Plexus composes these heterogeneous pieces into one differentiable system rather than requiring them to become one kind of entity or one numerical solver.

Reference — load path, Keene et al. (1987); adhesions are clusters at ~555 nm, Changede & Sheetz (2017); protease network, Karagiannis & Popel (2004), with the inhibitor held in the matrix rather than dissolved in it, Yu et al. (2000). Note: discovery_okuda/note_spheroid_bm_ecm.pdf.

Stigmergy — Physarum slime (one species, a disc colony)

A set of agents coupled to a scalar field: agents deposit a trail, the field diffuses and decays, agents sense and steer up it — a self-reinforcing transport network (one schedule: deposit → diffuse → decay → sense → steer). Here a single species is seeded in a disc (spawn: disc), so the colony stays a bounded circular web; the three differ only in turn rate and sensor geometry.

Reference — the model is Jones (2010), Artificial Life 16(2):127–153, whose sense-and-deposit agent this is; the implementation we adapted is Sebastian Lague’s Slime-Simulation, and the name for the mechanism is Grassé’s (1959) stigmergy.

Stigmergy — Physarum slime (many species, space-filling)

The same schedule, but agents are now seeded uniformly across the field (spawn: random) and split into 4–8 species, each depositing and sensing its own trail channel with cross-species inhibition (cross: −1). That is why this row looks so unlike the discs above: instead of one bounded colony, the networks fill the whole plane and segregate into distinct interwoven colours.

Reference — the model is Jones (2010), Artificial Life 16(2):127–153, whose sense-and-deposit agent this is; the implementation we adapted is Sebastian Lague’s Slime-Simulation, and the name for the mechanism is Grassé’s (1959) stigmergy.

Strange attractors

A single lateral operator rides a whole cloud along a dissipative 3D chaotic flow ẋ = f(x); a tiny seed is stretched-and-folded until the cloud is the attractor. Ten systems ship; six shown.

Reference — Lorenz (1963), J. Atmos. Sci. 20:130–141; Thomas (1999), Int. J. Bifurc. Chaos 9:1889–1905; Sprott (1994), Phys. Rev. E 50:R647–R650; Dadras & Momeni (2009), Phys. Lett. A 373:3637–3642. The Halvorsen and Aizawa systems have no primary paper we can point at — they are catalogued rather than published, Halvorsen in Sprott’s Chaos and Time-Series Analysis (2003) — and are named here as folklore rather than cited as literature.

Interaction & flocking

Lateral operators over a radius neighbour graph: a per-type force law (attraction–repulsion) and the three boids rules that sum to flocking.

Reference — boids is Reynolds (1987), SIGGRAPH Comput. Graph. 21(4):25–34, the soft-core pair potential is D’Orsogna et al. (2006), Phys. Rev. Lett. 96:104302, and the alignment model is Vicsek et al. (1995), Phys. Rev. Lett. 75:1226–1229; the implementations are adapted from ParticleGraph (Allier et al.).

Inverse-square law — self-gravity

One squared_law operator with law: gravity — mass, always attracting, summed over every pair. It collapses a disc into a galaxy, and because the sum runs over the whole set, two galaxies in one set pull on each other through that same operator: a collision needs no second law.

Reference — softened self-gravity after Philip Mocz’s Create Your Own N-body Simulation (2020); the encounter geometry after Toomre & Toomre, Galactic Bridges and Tails (1972).
