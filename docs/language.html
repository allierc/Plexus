Language

Entities, activities and their organization, written down to be run

A mechanism is entities and activities organized such that they are productive of regular changes from start or set-up to finish or termination conditions (Machamer, Darden & Craver, 2000, p. 3). Plexus is a language for writing a mechanism down in exactly those terms: the entities with their states and relations, the activities that act on them, and their organization in space, in time and across levels, so that the description can be run, fitted to observations, and shown to be missing an activity. Every activity names the entities it acts on, the state it needs, the relations between those entities, and one or more numerical implementations. The research programme has three objectives. First, an algebra of activities. Second, to decompose the existing corpus of simulation models into that language. Third, to build differentiable implementations of every activity, so that mechanistic models can be inferred from biological data.

Introduction

Computational biology has produced a rich ecosystem of simulation frameworks. Neural simulators model electrical activity, reaction–diffusion systems model chemical transport, particle and continuum methods model mechanics, while vertex, Cellular Potts and active-matter models describe multicellular tissues. These frameworks remain largely incompatible. Combining electrical signalling, mechanics, metabolism and development requires coupling several specialized simulators whose underlying abstractions differ fundamentally, and each simulator carries its own representation of what a mechanism is, so that models are hard to compare, compose and extend. This fragmentation is not primarily a numerical problem. It is a language problem.

Biology already has an account of what a mechanism is, even if it is rarely written out. In the account of Machamer, Darden and Craver, a mechanism has two halves. Entities are the things: they have properties and stand in spatial relations to one another. Activities are what the things do: they are the producers of change. And the two are organized. Spatially, by location, shape and contact. Temporally, by order, rate and duration. Hierarchically, because what the parts do at one level constitutes what the whole does at the next. A mechanism starts from set-up conditions and ends at termination conditions, and its description bottoms out in activities that the field takes as fundamental. This is the account a cell biologist uses tacitly whenever a paper ends with a summary figure: named parts, arrows between them, boxes inside boxes. The paper has exact words for that figure. A mechanism sketch is a description with gaps, black boxes that no known activity yet fills. A mechanism schema is an abstract description whose placeholders can all be filled with known parts and activities. A complete description exhibits productive continuity without gaps from set-up to termination conditions, and the continuity lies in the arrows.

Plexus takes that account literally. Instead of treating simulation frameworks as fundamental, it treats entities and activities as the primary objects. An activity, such as adhesion, diffusion, division, chemotaxis, electrical propagation or active stress, is written down independently of how it is computed. In a spec an activity is written as an operator; the two words name the same thing, and this page uses the paper’s. In this perspective biological modelling becomes analogous to compiler design. Just as a compiler separates a programming language from its implementations through an intermediate representation, Plexus separates the mechanism from its numerical realization.

Entities are heterogeneous, receptors, signalling proteins, cells, extracellular matrices, tissues, fields, and they are organized across levels. Plexus makes this organization explicit: each level carries its own entities and states, activities act between entities within a level, and aggregate ↑ and broadcast ↓ connect the levels by carrying state from parts to whole and from whole to parts. Decomposition therefore does not flatten a tissue into one collection of variables. It keeps the organization through which a mechanism at one level is composed with a mechanism at another.

The purpose of the decomposition is recomposition: to build complete mechanistic models from reusable entities and activities. Making such compositions executable requires a strong structural choice, and Plexus makes it by separating the language that states the hypothesis from the code that runs it. The language declares entities, states, relations, organization and activities; the interpreter resolves the declaration into numerical implementations and runs them in the declared order. What the language guarantees is that a hypothesis is well formed and complete. Whether it is productive of the change observed is what running it establishes.

The language

The description of a mechanism is built from five things, in this order: entities, the state each carries, the relations between them, the activities that act on them, and the organization that arranges all of these in space, in time and across levels. Fields are the environmental quantities among which the entities act, and an operator is how an activity is written in a specification. A model is a composition of activities over organized entities, not a simulator-specific algorithm.

Entities

Entities come in kinds, and each kind lives at one level of organization: genes, proteins, cells, neurons, synapses, tissues, organs, organisms. The kinds organize into hierarchies that reflect biological organization. Genes regulate proteins, proteins determine what a cell does, cells assemble into tissues, tissues into organs. Every kind is represented in the same way, so that one language describes intracellular signalling, multicellular mechanics, development and organ physiology. A kind need not be a whole organism’s worth of one thing: the vertices of a cell sheet are entities of one kind, the material points of a gel are entities of another, and both can be parts of the same cell.

States

Every entity carries a state describing its current condition. Entities say what exists; states say what each entity carries. The state schema is specific to each kind. A protein may carry a position, a binding state and the load it bears; a material point a position, a velocity and a deformation gradient; a neuron a membrane voltage and a calcium level; a cell a polarity, a stiffness and its gene-expression levels; a synapse a conductance and a plastic weight. Activities transform states: they read selected state variables and write updates to others, while the entities themselves remain what they were.

Fields

A field is a continuous quantity defined over space rather than over entities: a morphogen concentration, a pressure, an extracellular signal, an electric potential, a matrix density. Fields complement entities. Entities are the discrete parts, fields the continuous environment those parts live in, and activities couple the two through explicit maps from entities to positions in space.

Relations

Relations say which entities stand together: which protein belongs to which cell, which synapses connect which neurons, which vertices bound which cell, which cells are neighbours. Each is an explicit map. A part-to-whole map organizes levels; a many-to-many relation, such as a synapse or a half-edge, is carried as entities of its own kind with two such maps. Activities declare the relations they traverse, so the spatial organization of a mechanism is written with the activity rather than assumed by the code.

Activities

An activity is the fundamental object of the language. It represents a single mechanism, such as adhesion, chemotaxis, diffusion, electrical propagation, active stress, metabolism, growth or division, by a typed signature and one or more numerical implementations. The signature states what change the activity produces; the implementations state how it is computed. The signature declares

  • the entities the activity acts on,
  • the entities it changes,
  • the state variables it reads and the ones it writes,
  • the maps relating the participating entities.

The maps are the relations the mechanism needs. They say which protein belongs to which cell, which synapses connect which neurons, or how cells relate to a field. Because the relations are part of the signature, an activity is a typed morphism between kinds of entities, and the spatial organization of the mechanism is declared with the activity rather than assumed by the code.

The algebra of activities

The central hypothesis of Plexus is that a large fraction of the activities in the simulation literature can be composed from a small number of elementary kinds. Rather than introducing a new activity for every phenomenon, Plexus identifies a compact algebra that is reused across mechanics, signalling, metabolism, development and physiology. There are eight kinds, and here each is named first in a biologist’s words:

  • lateral: what neighbours do to each other among entities of one kind.
  • aggregate ↑: integration. A whole reads its parts.
  • broadcast ↓: top-down control. A whole imposes on its parts.
  • exchange: traffic between entities and their microenvironment.
  • field: the microenvironment’s own life. Diffusion, decay, reaction.
  • rewire: remodelling. The relations change, the entities do not.
  • structural: turnover. divide and die.
  • seed: the set-up conditions, written once and never again.

The first five primarily change state while preserving the underlying organization. Rewire changes the relations through which subsequent activities act. Structural changes the entities themselves. Seed establishes the initial conditions from which the other activities operate.

Together these kinds form the elementary vocabulary of Plexus.

Lateral. Entities of one kind act on one another through their relations: mechanical interaction between particles, adhesion between cells, electrical coupling between neurons, contact signalling or biochemical interaction.

Aggregate. A whole reads or integrates its parts: a cell’s geometry from its vertices, a neuron’s input from its synapses, or a tissue property from its constituent cells. It provides the upward direction of organization.

Broadcast. A whole influences its parts: a cell’s polarity acts on its cytoskeleton, a tissue constrains its cells, or a material property is assigned to the points belonging to a cell. Aggregate and broadcast together express bidirectional organization across levels.

Exchange. Entities interact with their environment: secretion, uptake, sensing, transport or mechanical loading. Numerically this may be represented as transfer between entities and a spatial field.

Field. A continuous environment undergoes its own dynamics: diffusion, decay, reaction or other field evolution.

Rewire. Relations are remodelled while the entities persist: neighbour exchange, junction turnover, synaptic remodelling or changes in a contact network. Because the relations determine which entities participate in later activities, rewire changes the organization of subsequent dynamics.

Structural. The composition of the system changes. Divide creates entities and die removes them. Proliferation, apoptosis and extrusion therefore change the organization itself rather than merely changing an entity’s state.

Seed. The mechanism is given its set-up condition: initial positions, concentrations, pre-patterns or populations. A seed acts only at initialization, so an initial condition cannot silently become a persistent boundary condition.

The distinction between these kinds is therefore biological as much as computational: Plexus separates change of state, change of relation, and change of entity composition.

Organization in time

A mechanism is also temporally organized. Plexus represents this organization with a schedule, an ordered collection of activities specifying both when they occur and how often they are evaluated.

Formally, one simulation step is the composition

\[\Phi = \mathcal{O}_n \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{O}_1,\]

whose repeated application generates the dynamics

\[x_{t+1} = \Phi(x_t),\]

where \(x\) contains the state of every entity and field.

The schedule therefore expresses the temporal organization of the mechanism: its order, rate and duration. Different activities can operate at different tempos. Mechanical interactions may run every step, while diffusion, growth or division occur less frequently. A biological model is consequently a multi-rate organization of activities, rather than a monolithic numerical solver.

Activities are pure transformations. They do not directly modify the state; each produces its contribution to the change. If activity \(\mathcal{O}_i\) produces \(\Delta_i\), the complete update is

\[\Delta = \sum_i \Delta_i,\]

before being passed to the numerical implementation.

This keeps activities independent and composable: adding a mechanism means adding an activity to the schedule. It also preserves the separation between biological semantics and numerical realization: the schedule describes the hypothesis, while the implementation describes how it is evaluated.

Organization across levels

Biological mechanisms are hierarchical. A cell contains parts; a tissue contains cells; an organism contains tissues. But a whole can contain several kinds of parts simultaneously.

Plexus represents this through explicit part-to-whole maps. A cell can contain receptors, signalling proteins, cytoskeletal elements and material points, each retaining its own state and activities. Different descriptions of the same biological object can therefore coexist rather than being forced into one representation.

Activities can operate within a level or connect levels. Within a cell, activities may connect receptors, signalling proteins and cytoskeletal elements. Across levels, aggregate ↑ constructs properties of a whole from its parts, while broadcast ↓ carries properties or constraints from the whole to its parts.

The two directions are complementary. Parts can determine the state of a whole, while the whole can constrain the behaviour of its parts. Both are explicit activities rather than implicit consequences of the software architecture.

Fields belong to levels as well. A protein concentration inside a cell, a morphogen field among cells and a mechanical field under a tissue can coexist as distinct objects. Exchange activities connect entities to the field at the appropriate level.

Proteins → cells → tissues is therefore not a pipeline of separate simulators. It is one compositional organization: entities of several kinds, connected by activities within and across levels.

The same eight activity kinds apply throughout. Adding another level of organization does not require another kind of computational machinery.

Numerical implementations

A mechanistic description eventually reaches activities that are taken as given. In Plexus, this is where the numerical implementation enters.

The activity specifies its biological meaning; an implementation provides one way of computing it. A single activity may therefore have several implementations satisfying the same typed signature. They may differ in numerical method, spatial discretization, solver or hardware while preserving the same biological interpretation.

This accommodates a broad range of methods. A mechanical activity may be implemented using a material point method, finite elements, a vertex model, a phase field, a Cellular Potts model or optimal transport. A signalling activity may use Hodgkin–Huxley equations, a graph neural network or a neural ordinary differential equation. A field activity may use finite differences, finite elements, a neural implicit representation or a spectral method.

The numerical implementation may also determine how the activity is integrated: first-order states such as concentrations or membrane voltages, second-order states such as mechanical accelerations, or explicit, implicit or adaptive solvers. These choices affect computational cost and accuracy, not the biological meaning of the activity.

This separation changes how models can be compared. Traditional frameworks compare complete simulators, often entangling biological assumptions with numerical algorithms. Plexus separates the two questions.

  • Which mechanism is being proposed? Compare the activities and their organization.
  • How is that mechanism computed? Compare the implementations.

Biological progress therefore corresponds to new activities or new compositions of activities, while numerical progress corresponds to better implementations of activities already expressed by the language. Existing simulation frameworks can consequently become complementary numerical backends rather than competing modelling paradigms.

Mechanistic inverse modelling

A formal language of mechanisms is useful not only for running models but for asking whether a proposed mechanism explains observations.

Rather than assuming a simulator and manually calibrating it, the objective is to infer parameters, spatial organization and constitutive laws from biological observations.

Plexus makes this possible by associating activities with differentiable implementations satisfying their typed signatures. Differentiability therefore becomes a property of the mechanistic representation rather than of one particular simulator. Gradients can propagate through a composition of explicit biological activities.

This changes the role of inverse modelling. A conventional approach assumes that the mechanism is already correct and searches for parameters that reproduce the observations. That works when the mechanism is complete, but can be misleading when an important activity is absent: parameter optimization may compensate for the missing mechanism.

In Plexus, parameters remain attached to explicit activities. Mechanical properties belong to mechanical activities, reaction rates to biochemical activities, conductances to signalling activities, and spatial fields to the activities that generate or modify them. Parameter estimation therefore preserves the mechanistic interpretation of the model.

Residuals acquire the same interpretation. A persistent mismatch does not necessarily mean that the parameters are wrong. It may mean that the current composition of activities cannot produce the observed change.

In the language of mechanism sketches, the residual may point to a missing activity: a black box in the explanatory structure that has not yet been filled. Differentiability therefore does more than make optimization efficient. It allows competing mechanistic hypotheses to be compared quantitatively while preserving their explicit organization.

Agentic mechanistic discovery

Writing mechanisms as explicit activities turns discovery from a parameter-optimization problem into a language-exploration problem. Because the activities, entities and relations are explicit rather than buried in simulator-specific code, they can be inspected and manipulated by an agent.

Plexus proposes a three-loop strategy, moving from existing models toward new biological knowledge.

Loop I: Understanding. An existing simulation model is decomposed into Plexus entities, activities and organization. The agent identifies what exists, what changes, which relations are required, and how the existing code realizes each activity. Simulation frameworks therefore become repositories of reusable mechanisms.

Loop II: Fitting. The resulting mechanistic model is fitted to experimental observations. Parameters, fields and set-up conditions are estimated while remaining attached to explicit activities and organizational structure.

Loop III: Discovery. The remaining residual is analysed after fitting. A persistent discrepancy suggests that the present mechanism cannot produce the observed change. The agent proposes alternative activities or organizations, compares competing mechanistic hypotheses, and extends the algebra when the existing vocabulary is insufficient.

The three loops make simulation, inverse modelling and hypothesis generation successive stages of one scientific workflow. The language itself becomes an object of discovery: new biological knowledge can appear as a new activity, a new relation, a new organization, or a new composition of mechanisms, not merely as another parameter value.
